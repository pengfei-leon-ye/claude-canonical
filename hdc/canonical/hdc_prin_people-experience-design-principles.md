# People Experience Design Principles

- **Status:** transformed chat-project replica; not SOT
- **Active source(s):** `2_topics/hr-digital/people-experience-principles.md`
- **Conversation boundary:** guides discussion of candidate, employee, manager, HRBP, and alumni experience; it does not approve a service, policy, design, or organizational change

## 1. Journey-first, not function-first

Begin with a lifecycle stage and a person’s desired outcome. Connect policy, Process, content, service, and system around that moment.

Red flags:

- the structure follows HR department boundaries only;
- a person must understand internal system ownership to complete a simple task;
- one moment is fragmented without deliberate orchestration.

## 2. Moments that matter first

Prioritize moments by:

- frequency;
- emotional intensity;
- business value;
- risk;
- friction;
- transition significance.

Do not give every moment equal design depth or turn the journey catalog into an undifferentiated backlog.

## 3. User lens before HR lens

Describe the moment from the candidate, employee, manager, HRBP, or alumni perspective before translating it into internal terms.

Ask:

- What is the person trying to do?
- What do they need to know?
- What should they feel or trust?
- What would make the outcome feel fair and understandable?

## 4. Persona and context over the “average employee”

Overlay the shared journey with material context:

- worker type;
- role and manager context;
- location and language;
- work arrangement;
- accessibility needs;
- life or career transition;
- local legal or cultural constraints.

Treat a difference as a variant only when it changes the experience outcome or required control.

## 5. Digital where digital is best, human where human is best

Use digital service for speed, consistency, access, and straightforward guidance. Use human interaction for empathy, interpretation, discretion, conflict, and high-impact or emotionally loaded moments.

Make the switch between digital and human support clear, short, and dignified.

## 6. Manager-enabled experience by design

Many employee outcomes depend on the manager. Discuss the manager experience explicitly:

- what the manager must see, know, decide, or prepare;
- what prompts or guidance are needed;
- what belongs to the manager, HR, and employee respectively;
- how to avoid transferring avoidable administrative burden to the manager or employee.

## 7. Transition quality matters

Entry, internal move, promotion, leave and return, mobility, and exit are transitions rather than isolated transactions.

Discuss:

- readiness;
- clarity;
- continuity;
- support;
- ramp-up or re-entry;
- emotional load;
- follow-through after the formal event.

## 8. Continuous listening and adaptation

Experience design should include signals and a management response.

Use a balanced set of:

- experience signals;
- friction and service signals;
- adoption and utilization signals;
- manager and HRBP observations;
- outcome and risk signals.

Feedback without a response path is not an experience mechanism.

## 9. Global core journey with governed local variance

Define the shared lifecycle and moment first. Add local or persona variance only when it is mandatory, economically justified, or materially changes usability, accessibility, dignity, or compliance.

Each approved variance should have a reason, owner, affected moment, and review condition.

## 10. Make experience logic explicit

For a priority moment, make clear:

- lifecycle stage and moment;
- user lens and desired outcome;
- personas and context;
- manager and HRBP implications;
- digital-versus-human routing;
- accessibility and localization implications;
- service recovery or escalation;
- signals and intended management response;
- assumptions, open questions, and decisions needed.

Use only the structure needed to make the experience logic reviewable. A formal artifact is not required.

## 11. Preserve dignity when AI participates

AI assistance can feel supportive or evaluative depending on design.

Discuss:

- whether the person can recognize the AI, human, or hybrid role;
- what remains subject to human judgment;
- how a person can question or correct an outcome;
- how emotional load changes the need for human support;
- whether the fallback preserves context rather than forcing repetition;
- tone, timing, acknowledgment, and explanation;
- whether monitoring could be experienced as surveillance.

AI should not quietly withdraw support from high-emotion or high-impact moments.

## Experience discussion check

Before recommending an experience direction, confirm:

- the journey stage and moment are explicit;
- the user outcome is written in plain language;
- priority is justified;
- affected personas and local context are visible;
- the manager role is explicit where material;
- digital and human roles are deliberate;
- transitions, accessibility, localization, and dignity are addressed proportionately;
- feedback signals connect to action;
- unresolved policy, data, or control questions remain visible.
